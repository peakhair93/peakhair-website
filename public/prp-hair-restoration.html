The short answer

What is PRP for hair loss?

PRP stands for platelet-rich plasma. A clinician takes a small blood sample, separates and concentrates its platelets, then injects the preparation into selected areas of the scalp. Unlike a transplant, PRP does not move follicles into bald areas. Research suggests that some people with pattern hair loss may see an improvement in hair density, but results and treatment methods vary. First find out why you are losing hair: sudden shedding, a scalp condition or a medical cause may call for a different plan.

Sources: American Academy of Dermatology on diagnosing hair loss and systematic review of PRP in pattern hair loss.

Why consider it

Potential advantages, without promises

No graft harvesting

PRP is an injection-based treatment. It does not require removing donor follicles or designing a surgical hairline.

Uses your own blood

Platelets come from your blood sample. That does not make the injections risk-free; screening and careful technique still matter.

An option for existing hair

It may be discussed when follicles are still present and thinning. It cannot be relied upon to rebuild a fully absent hairline or replace a transplant.

PRP is not a cure for every kind of hair loss. A review found signals of improved hair density in some studies, but the evidence was limited by differences in protocols and study quality. Ask your clinician how this applies to your diagnosis.

The treatment journey

What a PRP plan involves

Establish the cause

Discuss when thinning began, medical history and scalp symptoms. A clinician may recommend additional evaluation rather than injections.

Prepare and inject

Blood is collected, processed to separate platelet-rich plasma and injected into the areas selected in your treatment plan.

Review the response

If a series is recommended, agree on the number and spacing of visits, a way to document change and the point at which to reconsider the plan.

Aftercare: You may have temporary tenderness, redness or bruising from the blood draw or scalp injections. Follow the clinician's instructions about washing, exercise and medicines; do not assume there is zero downtime for everyone.

Who might consider PRP?

Diagnose before treating

PRP has been studied most often for male and female pattern hair loss. A clinician can explain whether it is worth considering alongside established treatments. If your hair loss is sudden, patchy, associated with itching or inflammation, or follows a medical or life event, the underlying cause needs attention first.

Some forms of shedding can improve without PRP. Do not assume that every kind of hair loss, including postpartum or medication-associated shedding, will respond to the same injection plan. Read the American Academy of Dermatology's diagnosis guidance →

Patient questions

Clear answers about PRP

How many PRP sessions will I need?

There is no single schedule appropriate for everyone. Peak offers multi-session packages, but the number and timing should be confirmed after your assessment, with a plan for follow-up and reassessment.

When will I see a change?

Hair changes take months, not days. Results are variable and some patients see no meaningful improvement. Agree on consistent photographs and a review date with your clinician rather than a guaranteed timeline.

Is PRP better than a hair transplant?

They address different needs. PRP is non-surgical and may help existing thinning hair; FUE transfers follicles to a planned area. Neither should be selected before diagnosis and an assessment of your goals.

Can PRP be used after a transplant?

Peak discusses PRP as a possible adjunct. Timing and suitability should be decided by the treating surgeon. PRP cannot guarantee transplanted graft survival or a particular density.

Is there downtime or discomfort?

There is no surgical donor wound, but blood collection and scalp injections can cause temporary soreness, redness or bruising. Ask for instructions specific to your treatment.
